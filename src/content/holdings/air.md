AAR Corporation (AIR) fell 7.2% the day it detailed its plan to buy 65%
of MRO Holdings, and the market has treated the deal as an overpayment
ever since. The market is half right. AAR paid at least a full price: on
my numbers, MRO’s return stays below AAR’s roughly 10% cost of capital
through fiscal 2030, even with the synergies. Where the market is wrong
is the multiple. It still values the combined company like the old AAR,
even though the deal nearly triples AAR’s airframe capacity and lifts
margins toward peers that trade at almost twice its multiple. That
re-rating, not a cheap deal, is why my 12-month target is about \$157. I
bought shares at about \$97.50 on Oct 26, when I started this research.

MRO Holdings is an airframe heavy maintenance company with five sites in
El Salvador, Mexico, Colombia, Florida, and North Carolina. It expects
\$1.0 billion in sales in 2026 at a 27% EBITDA margin. The deal values
MRO at \$4 billion in enterprise value, about 10.7x its 2026 EBITDA,
including synergies. AAR will fund it with \$2.1 billion of new debt,
\$780 million of AAR stock issued to the sellers at \$135 a share, and a
\$230 million private placement led by the Pritzker Organization. It’s
expected to close by February 2027. The market’s main worry is the debt,
with net leverage rising to about 3.6x at closing. Shares sit at \$97.34
(as of Oct 26), down from \$153.71 in August. Below, I show that most of
that decline was sector-wide, that the debt is manageable, and that the
combined company deserves a higher multiple than the market is paying.

## Company Background

AAR, based in Wood Dale, Illinois, sells parts and services to airlines,
cargo operators, aircraft lessors and governments, mostly after aircraft
are delivered. Parts Supply, 45% of fiscal 2026 sales, distributes new
parts for manufacturers and resells used parts, and holds most of AAR’s
inventory. Repair, Engineering and Software, 33%, runs AAR’s airframe
hangars and component repair shops and sells maintenance software.
Government Solutions, 15%, supports defense customers, and Legacy
Commercial Programs, 7%, holds older commercial contracts that AAR
reports separately. Commercial customers made up 72% of fiscal 2026
sales. AAR gets paid per part sold, per hour or package of repair work,
and under multi-year supply contracts.

**Management and ownership.** John Holmes has been CEO since 2018 and
chairman since 2023. He joined AAR in 2001 in mergers and acquisitions
and ran operations before becoming CEO. Dylan Wolin became CFO in
February 2026, returning after leading AAR’s corporate development,
treasury and investor relations from 2017 to 2024. Insiders own about
2.5% of the shares. After closing, MRO’s sellers will hold about 5.8
million AAR shares, roughly 12% of the company, released from lockup in
thirds over 18 months.

Pay leans on earnings growth. Holmes’s fiscal 2026 bonus was tied 80% to
adjusted EPS and 20% to net working capital turns, and it paid out at
180% of target. His performance shares, 60% of his stock awards, vest
70% on adjusted income, 20% on average ROIC and 10% on relative
shareholder return. That’s a mild concern for this deal. MRO adds to EPS
even though its return (8.5% by fiscal 2030) stays below my 10.05% cost
of capital, so management can earn full pay before shareholders earn
theirs. The working-capital metric helps, since it ties the bonus to the
cash conversion I’m watching.

## The Selloff Was Mostly the Sector, Not the Deal

Most of AIR’s decline since August has had little to do with the MRO
deal. From Aug 17 to Sept 28, AIR fell 25.1%, about the same as VSE
Corp. (VSEC) at 28.3%, while HEICO (HEI) dropped 18.0%. Airlines fell
too, but by much less: the US Global Jets ETF (JETS) lost 7.6%, as oil
rose about 15%. That means aftermarket stocks fell roughly 3 times
harder than the airlines they service. One possible reason is that high
fuel prices push airlines to retire older, less fuel-efficient jets
sooner, and those older jets need heavy maintenance. Investors may have
priced in weaker future demand for exactly the work AAR does.

Deal day is the cleanest read on what the market thinks of the MRO deal.
On Sept. 29, the first trading day after AAR reported earnings and
announced the deal, AIR fell 7.2% while VSE slipped 1.8% and HEICO rose
0.8%. Alongside the deal, AAR also posted adjusted EPS up 38% year over
year to \$1.49. Strong earnings usually lift a stock, but AIR fell
anyway. Since its peers barely moved, the 5–8-point gap was the market’s
verdict on the deal itself.

The scare: AAR will raise \$2.1B in new debt to acquire MRO. That is
more than double the \$885 million it carries today, lifting net
leverage from about 2.0x to 3.6x at closing, by management’s estimate,
including synergies. If AAR borrows at about 7%, close to the 6.75% on
its existing notes, the new debt costs about \$112 million a year after
tax, equal to roughly 60% of AAR’s [fiscal 2026 net income of \$187.7
million](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000001750/000110465926085429/tm2620584d1_ex99-1.htm).
Part of it may also float: on Oct 26, [AAR enlarged its variable rate
credit
line](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000001750/000110465926118655/tm2628131d2_ex99-1.htm)
to \$1.375 billion to help pay for the deal. For a company with a 12%
EBITDA margin, investors see little cushion if demand softens. As I show
below, that fear is overstated because cash flow, not interest rates,
decides how fast the debt comes down.

Once the deal-day selling ended, AIR traded in line with its peers,
which makes the deal-specific penalty small and measurable. Volume ran
two to seven times normal for five trading days after the announcement,
so I measure the reaction from Sept 28 to Oct 5. Over that stretch, AIR
fell 11.2%, while VSE, which made no acquisition, fell 3.7% and HEICO
fell 1.0%, a gap of about 7-9 points. On AAR’s pre-deal market value of
about \$4.6 billion, that’s roughly \$340-400 million. Because AAR
posted strong earnings at the same time, the true penalty may be
somewhat larger. After Oct 5, AIR fell 4.8% through Oct 26, in line with
the 4.7% average for VSE and HEICO. For a deal this size, that’s a
modest haircut, and I think a fair one. AAR paid about 13.5x MRO’s
EBITDA before synergies, so the \$75 million of synergies mostly earns
back the price rather than adding to it, as the return math in the
valuation section shows. The mispricing isn’t the deal. It’s the
multiple the market still puts on the company the deal creates. So what
did AAR actually buy?

## AAR Is Buying Scale It Couldn’t Build Fast Enough

The deal gives AAR something it has been trying to build one hangar at a
time: scale. AAR’s annual service hours will jump from 7 million to
about 19 million, the aircraft it services each year from about 1,200 to
nearly 3,000, and its facilities from 7 to 12. MRO alone runs 115
maintenance lines. In other words, the deal nearly triples AAR's
airframe business overnight. Scale matters because airlines often send
whole fleets to one provider, and more lines should let AAR bid on
contracts it couldn’t handle before. Building that much capacity on its
own would take AAR years, as its recent expansions show.

The MRO deal is the biggest of several capacity moves AAR has made in
the past year. AAR has been adding space both ways: expanding its own
hangars in Oklahoma City and Miami, which should add about \$60 million
of annual revenue at full run rate in fiscal 2027, and [buying HAECO
Americas for \$77
million](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/01/06/aar-air-q2-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)
in November 2025. HAECO will add about 40% more heavy maintenance
capacity over time, while MRO’s 12 million service hours add about 170%
to AAR’s 7 million. Three capacity moves in a year point to demand
outrunning space, and building at MRO’s scale one hangar at a time would
take years. HAECO is still being integrated, and it is weighing on
margins for now.

MRO isn’t just more hangar capacity. It’s a more profitable business
that makes more money on every dollar of work. MRO’s annual service
hours grew from about 10 million in 2024 to 12 million in 2026, roughly
9% a year, while its EBITDA grew about 20% a year. [Bain’s 2024
release](https://baincapital.com/news/leading-aviation-maintenance-provider-mro-holdings-secures-strategic-growth-investment-bain)
describes both organic and strategic growth, so part of that 20% likely
came from acquisitions. Even so, MRO’s 27% EBITDA margin compares with
AAR’s repair segment operating margin of 7.8% in fiscal 2026. EBITDA
margins run higher than operating margins because EBITDA leaves out
depreciation, so the true gap is somewhat smaller, but it’s still large.
When profit grows about twice as fast as volume, that points to
efficiency or pricing power, not just more planes. That’s what AAR is
paying for, and the upside is AAR’s own hangar moving toward MRO’s
standard.

MRO’s edge comes from three things: lower labor costs, faster
turnarounds and better systems. [On AAR’s Sept. 29 earnings
call](https://earningswhispers.com/transcript/AIR/Q12027), Holmes said
MRO has a “significant labor cost advantage, which is a key contributor
to their margin profile,” and added that AAR doesn’t see wage pressure
in MRO’s markets. Cheap labor is a real part of the story, and
management says so, which makes MRO’s margin vulnerable to the offshore
maintenance rules discussed in the risks section. Holmes also describes
MRO’s turnaround times as industry leading. Speed is capacity: every day cut from a heavy check frees a hangar line for the next plane, with no new
building needed. Holmes cautioned that MRO’s turnaround times aren’t
directly comparable to AAR’s, because the two handle different mixes of
work, so I don’t put a number on the speed advantage. Finally, MRO runs
proprietary operating systems that Holmes expects to lift margins at
both companies. If those systems spread to AAR’s hangars, that
improvement isn’t in the \$75 million synergy target.

The deal also lets AAR shift out of its most expensive hangar and grow
where costs are lowest. AAR plans to fully exit Indianapolis, which
Holmes called its highest-cost facility, by the end of calendar 2026.
MRO recently opened its seventh hangar in El Salvador, which Holmes
called “the largest hangar in the world,” with an eighth already
planned. Holmes also said AAR would look to expand meaningfully into
widebody maintenance at MRO’s sites, as airlines move widebody work from
Asia to the Americas. AAR’s mix shifts from its most expensive hangar
toward its cheapest, and widebodies open a larger market from the
combined business. Neither is in the \$75 million synergy target, and
growing in El Salvador also adds exposure to the offshore maintenance
rules covered in the risks section.

In my view, the largest upside may not be in the hangars at all, but in
the parts that go into them. Parts supply is AAR’s fastest-growing
business; sales rose 31% to \$414.8 million last quarter at a 13.3%
operating margin, with new parts distribution up 23% organically. Every
heavy check uses parts, and MRO runs about 12 million hours of checks a
year. Management left that out of its numbers. [The deal presentation
says](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000001750/000110465926111482/tm2626100d3_ex99-2.htm)
cross-selling is “not captured in plan,” and the \$75 million target
covers cost savings only. Holmes named component repair as the main
cross-selling opportunity on the call: parts are my extension of his
point. Every \$10 million of added EBITDA would be worth about \$3 per
share at 15x. Some airlines supply their own parts for heavy checks,
though, so it isn’t yet known how much of MRO’s own work AAR could
capture. Scale, efficiency and cross-selling are how margins rise, and
the next section measures how far.

## The Margin Target Depends More on AAR Than on MRO

Management’s 19-20% margin target needs more than its promised
synergies. On AAR’s own pro forma figures for fiscal 2026, the combined
company would have earned \$686 million of EBITDA on \$4.3 billion of
sales at a 15.9% margin. That’s up from AAR’s standalone 12.1%, but the
jump comes from adding MRO’s higher-margin business, not from anything
the combination achieves. Management aims for 19-20% within three to
four years. Adding the full \$75 million of synergies gets it to about
17.6%, a 1.3-2.3-point shortfall or roughly \$55-100 million of EBITDA a
year. And because the \$75 million is fixed while sales keep growing, it
moves the margin less each year: by fiscal 2030, only about 1.3 points.
The rest must come from MRO outgrowing AAR, AAR’s margin gains, or
cross-selling. Management doesn’t show the bridge, so I built it.

In my base case, the combined margin reaches 18.8% by fiscal 2030, just
short of management's target. Starting from 15.9%, AAR’s own margin
gains add 1.4 points, synergies add 1.3, and MRO’s fast growth adds 0.2
as its higher margin sales become a bigger share of the total. The
biggest piece isn’t the synergies; it’s AAR improving its own business.
That assumes AAR grows sales 8% a year and lifts its margin to 14%, the
top of management’s standalone target; MRO grows 10% a year at its
current margin, and I count the full \$75 million of synergies at run
rate, as management does for its target, with nothing for cross-selling.
The deck labels MRO’s own margin 27%, but its figures (\$285 million on
\$1.0 billion of sales) imply 28.5%, which is what I use. MRO’s calendar
year figures are combined with AAR’s fiscal year ending May 31, as in
the deck. The chart below shows the bridge.

Two things could close the gap, and the more powerful one is in AAR’s
own hangars, not MRO’s. With everything else unchanged, MRO growing 15%
a year lifts the margin to about 19.3%, while AAR reaching a 15% margin
lifts it to about 19.6%. Each point of AAR margin adds about 0.75 points
to the combined margin, more than five extra points a year of MRO growth
(about 0.45). The same lever cuts the other way: if AAR stalls at 13%,
the combined margin reaches only about 18.1%. Cross-selling would help
too, since just \$11 million of extra EBITDA by fiscal 2030 would close
the base case gap on its own. The target is reachable, but it depends
more on AAR running its own hangars better than on the deal itself.

The base case isn’t a stretch because AAR’s margin gain is partly a
recovery. AAR’s repair segment margin fell to 5.4% last quarter from
8.8% a year earlier as the HAECO integration temporarily diluted it, and
management warned of 12 to 18 months of dilution when it bought HAECO.
Part of the 1.4-point gain from AAR’s own margin is just getting back to
where it was, which makes the base case more believable. The bear case
is different. If the offshore maintenance rules push MRO’s margin down
to 24%, AAR's own margin stalls near 12.5%, and it delivers only 60% of
its synergies, the combined margin reaches just 16% by fiscal 2030,
barely above where it starts. In that case, the deal benefit is the
one-time jump from combining, and little after. Margin drives cash flow,
which the next section shows decides how fast AAR pays down the debt.

## The Debt Is Manageable If Cash Conversion Improves

The debt is what scared investors on deal day, but in my base case it
comes down quickly. Net debt to EBITDA is about 3.8x at closing, 3.1x in
fiscal 2028, 2.4x in 2029, and 1.9x in 2030. My closing figures are
higher than management’s 3.6x because I leave out synergies that haven’t
arrived yet. Management targets about 3.0x within two years and 2.0-2.5x
over time, so by fiscal 2029 the base case is already inside its
longer-term range. That path depends on AAR turning its EBITDA into
cash, which is the real risk, as I show below.

Interest rates were part of the deal-day worry, but even a large rise
barely changes the debt path. I assume about 7% on the new debt, close
to the 6.75% on AAR’s existing notes. A 2-point rise on the new and
floating debt adds only about \$47 million of net debt by fiscal 2030,
lifting leverage from 1.91x to 1.95x. The model assumes about half the
new debt floats; even if all of it did, the hit would only be about
0.08x. Each point of rates costs about \$16 million a year after tax on
\$2.1 billion, small next to the roughly \$770 million of EBITDA the
combined company should earn at closing. There are caveats: the revolver
floats at SOFR plus 1.25-2.25%, about 5.2-6.2% today, and the 6.75%
notes mature in March 2029 and will need refinancing. Refinancing them 2
points higher would add about \$14 million a year before tax. This is a
stress test, not a rate forecast, and it shows rates are a nuisance, not
a threat. The real risk is cash conversion.

The debt path depends on how much of AAR’s EBITDA turns into cash, and
that’s been weak. In fiscal 2026, only about 28% of AAR’s EBITDA became
cash before interest: adjusted operating cash of \$94 million, plus
after-tax interest, less \$37 million of capex on \$401 million of
EBITDA. That’s largely because the fast-growing parts business ties up
cash in inventory. My base case assumes only a slight improvement to
30%. That’s conservative: my DCF, which ties working capital to
inventory days, implies AAR converts closer to half its EBITDA at 8%
growth, so the debt could come down faster than shown here. If AAR’s
conversion instead falls to 22% and MRO’s margin slips to 24%, leverage
stays above 3.0x even in fiscal 2030. By fiscal 2029, the bear case sits
at 3.4x versus 2.4x in the base case, a gap of about 1.0x, roughly 25
times the effect of the 2-point rate rise. Two things help: MRO
generated \$203 million of adjusted operating cash flow in 2025, about
71% of its expected 2026 EBITDA, with maintenance capex of only about 1%
of sales, and AAR keeps all of MRO’s excess cash for the first two years
before the sellers’ 35% share kicks in. AAR’s next report, expected in late December or early January, is the first check on cash conversion. The chart
below shows the path under each case.

Management says the deal will add to adjusted EPS in its first full
fiscal year after closing, which is fiscal 2028. That’s true, but only
barely unless the synergies arrive early. My baseline is AAR’s

synergies, the deal cuts EPS by about 3.2%; with 25%, it adds 1.3%; with
all of them, it adds 14.6%. Management expects the full run rate within
three to four years of closing, so only a fraction should show up in
fiscal 2028. I assume about 25%, which means the deal adds only about 1%
EPS that year. The drag is structural: EPS counts only AAR’s 65% of
MRO’s profit, while AAR pays all of the interest, about \$112 million
after tax, and issues about 7.5 million new shares. In the early years,
EPS depends on how fast synergies arrive, so watch management’s progress

conversion improves and synergies arrive on schedule, leverage falls

whether today’s share price already prices in that risk.

## At Under 13x, the Market Is Still Pricing the Old AAR

I value AIR on EV/EBITDA because it compares companies regardless of how
much debt they carry, and this deal significantly changed AAR’s debt.
The calculations start with combined EBITDA of \$660.6 million: AAR’s
trailing \$375.6 million plus MRO’s expected 2026 figure of \$285
million. Applying a multiple gives enterprise value. From that, I
subtract net debt at closing of \$2,880.5 million, then the 35% of MRO
that the sellers keep, valued at the same multiple, since that slice
belongs to them, not to AAR’s shareholders. Dividing what’s left by
about 47.4 million shares after the deal gives a per-share value. For
AAR and its peers, I use reported trailing EBITDA from Finviz, which is
lower than AAR’s adjusted figure, so the valuation leans conservative.

The market still values AAR as if the deal hadn’t happened. The two most
profitable peers, HEICO and FTAI Aviation (FTAI), earn EBITDA margins
near 30% and trade at 32.0x and 19.4x. VSE, at a 16% margin, trades at
23.5x, and even AerSale (ASLE), at just 8%, trades at 16.7x. AAR, at
11%, trades at 12.7x, well below the peer median of about 21.5x. Part of
that discount is earned since investors pay more for higher margins. But
combined with MRO, AAR’s margin rises to about 14.7%, close to VSE’s.
Yet at \$97.34, the market values the combined company at about 12.6x,
slightly below the 12.7x it puts on AAR alone. The chart below plots
each company’s multiple against its margin.

I use 15x because it’s roughly where AAR itself traded before the deal.
On the same Finviz basis, AAR was at about 14.6x on Sept 28, before the
deal-day drop, and near 19x in mid-August. So 15x only puts the combined
company back at AAR’s pre-deal multiple, before giving any credit for
adding a business with more than twice its margin. It’s also
conservative against peers: VSE has a similar margin and trades at
23.5x, so 15x still values AAR about 35% below VSE. Debt doesn’t explain
the discount either. On Finviz’s figures, VSE’s enterprise value is
about \$950 million more than its market value, implying net debt of
roughly 4.4x EBITDA, more than AAR’s 3.8x at closing. Yet VSE trades at
23.5x. That’s an approximation from enterprise value less market cap,
but it’s enough to show leverage isn’t what’s holding AAR’s multiple
down. I’m not using HEICO’s 32x or the 21.5x peer median. HEICO earns
its premium from proprietary parts, while AAR still has to prove the
integration and the cash conversion. At \$97.34, the stock trades just
below \$99, its value at 12.7x with no synergies, so the market is
pricing the combined company below AAR’s post-selloff multiple and
giving it none of the synergies.

My 12-month target is about \$157, roughly 61% above the Oct 26 price of
\$97.34. It gives equal weight to two methods. The first is the
multiple. At 15x, the combined company’s \$735.6 million of EBITDA,
including the full \$75 million of synergies, implies an enterprise
value of about \$11.0 billion; subtracting \$2.88 billion of net debt
and \$1.07 billion for the sellers’ 35% leaves about \$7.1 billion of
equity, or roughly \$150 per share on 47.4 million shares. That counts
all the synergies, which management expects within three to four years,
so it isn’t a 12-month value. Applying the same 15x to where my base
case puts EBITDA a year from now, about \$790 million on the same basis
as the peers, less about \$2.77 billion of net debt and the sellers’ 35%
valued at the same multiple, gives about \$164.

The second is a 10-year discounted cash flow model, which tests whether
the business’s cash flows support that multiple. At a 10.05% cost of
capital, built from a 5.0% 10-year Treasury yield and the betas of AAR
and four peers, and 3% long-term growth, it values AIR at about \$134
today, or about \$150 a year from now. The DCF’s enterprise value works
out to about 15.6x today’s combined EBITDA, so the cash flows support
the 15x multiple I use. They don’t support it forever: a 15x multiple
ten years out would imply 5.7% growth in perpetuity, so I don’t use the
DCF’s exit-multiple version. Averaging \$164 and \$150 gives my target
of about \$157.

The stock doesn’t need everything to go right. With no synergies,
today’s EBITDA at 15x gives about \$126 (29% upside), and with half of
them, about \$138 (42%). Even at AAR’s post-selloff 12.7x, full
synergies would be worth about \$119 a share, 22% upside, and with no

One more check keeps the bull case honest: on returns, AAR paid a full
price for MRO. To test whether AAR overpaid, I divide MRO’s after-tax
operating profit plus after-tax synergies by the \$3.85 billion AAR is
effectively paying (\$4.0 billion less about \$150 million of tax
benefits). With full synergies, the return starts at about 6.5% and
rises to about 8.5% by fiscal 2030 in my base case, still below AAR’s
cost of capital of about 10%. In the bear case, it reaches only about
5.7%; in the bull case, about 10%. MRO doesn’t disclose its
depreciation, so the model assumes 3% of sales. So AAR didn’t get a
bargain: in my base case, the deal earns its cost of capital only if MRO
keeps growing past fiscal 2030. But both things can be true at once. AAR
paid a full price for MRO, yet the market still values the combined
company at AAR’s post-selloff multiple. The upside in my target comes
from that re-rating, not from the deal being cheap.

## The Risks Center on MRO’s Margin

The thesis has some real risks, and the biggest ones hit the same thing:
MRO’s margin.

Offshore maintenance: Labor costs are a key part of MRO’s margin, and
Washington is tightening oversight of foreign repair stations. Under the
[2024 FAA Reauthorization
Act](https://arsa.org/faa-reauthorization-2024/), [airline reporting of
foreign heavy
maintenance](https://www.faa.gov/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/info/all_infos/InFO26012.pdf)
and drug screening are already in force. Still awaiting codification are
Part 65 licensing for sign-off staff, surprise inspections, and extra
oversight of weaker countries. The FAA [hasn’t said which countries’
licensing will count as equivalent](https://arsa.org/frs/); if El
Salvador, Mexico, or Colombia don’t qualify, MRO would need more costly
U.S.-certified staff for sign-off roles. Each point of MRO margin is
worth about \$10 million of EBITDA, or about \$2 a share at 15x after
the sellers’ 35% share, so a 5-point hit would cost about \$10 a share.
Two offsets: MRO’s two U.S. sites aren’t affected, and Holmes says AAR
sees no wage pressure in MRO’s markets, though that’s management’s view.

Cash conversion: As shown above, only about 28% of AAR’s fiscal 2026
EBITDA turned into cash before interest, largely because its
fast-growing parts business ties up cash in inventory. If that doesn’t
improve, leverage stays above management’s 3.0x target even in fiscal
2030. Slower paydown would leave less room to buy the rest of MRO and
would likely delay the re-rating my target depends on. AAR’s next report is the first test.

A demand peak: Part of today’s demand exists because Boeing and Airbus
are delivering new jets late, so airlines keep older planes flying, and
older planes need more heavy checks. When deliveries catch up, or if
higher fuel prices push airlines to retire less efficient jets early,
that demand could ease. Heavy checks and parts ride the same tailwind,
so both would slow together, possibly while AAR still carries elevated
debt. If MRO’s growth stalled entirely, the 2030 margin would reach
about 18.0% instead of 18.8%. Longer term, the deal presentation
projects the global fleet growing from 29,000 to 42,000 aircraft by
2035, which should cushion the downturn.

Integration: This is AAR’s seventh acquisition in four years, and the
last one isn’t finished. HAECO is still diluting the repair margin,
which fell to 5.4% from 8.8%, with recovery promised by about mid-2027.
MRO is roughly 50 times HAECO’s \$77 million price, so the stakes are
far higher. If AAR delivers only 60% of the \$75 million in synergies,
the shortfall costs about \$9.50 a share at 15x. On the positive side,
Holmes said AAR has already dropped unprofitable work at HAECO and
replaced it with better contracts.

Deal and financing terms: The deal still needs regulatory approval, and
the permanent debt hasn’t been priced; AAR’s \$700 million of 6.75%
notes also mature in March 2029. AAR can buy the sellers’ remaining
stake in stages: 30% in three 10% tranches on the second, third and
fourth anniversaries of closing, plus 5% at any time. The price is tied
to AAR’s own trailing EV/EBITDA multiple, with a floor of 13.5x and a
cap of 15.25x. At the deal value, each 10% would cost about \$277
million, roughly 0.3x of leverage, and more if MRO keeps growing. That
creates a catch for my thesis: if AAR re-rates toward 15x, the rest of
MRO gets more expensive. The sellers will also own about 12% of AAR, and
their lock-ups expire in thirds at 6, 12 and 18 months after closing, so
their selling could weigh on the shares.

Customer concentration: About 90% of MRO’s sales go to U.S. customers,
mostly large airlines, so losing a major contract or a customer in
financial trouble would hurt. Those same airlines must now report their
foreign heavy maintenance to the FAA, and if that scrutiny pushes them
to bring work back home, MRO’s Latin American hangars would feel it
first.

## Bottom Line: A Buy for the Re-Rating

The market is right that AAR paid a full price for MRO Holdings, but
it’s wrong to value the combined company at AAR’s post-selloff multiple.
Most of AIR’s decline since August was sector-wide, the deal nearly
triples AAR’s capacity at higher margins, and the added debt is
manageable. Yet the stock trades at about 12.6x EBITDA. At 15x, roughly
AAR’s own pre-deal multiple, and backed by a DCF that supports that
multiple today, my 12-month target is about \$157, and even today’s
EBITDA with no synergies is worth about \$126. I rate AIR a Buy.

This is a two- to three-year idea, not a quick trade. The synergies take
time to arrive, and the market is unlikely to re-rate AAR until the
combined results show up in its numbers. Investors should expect
volatility around the closing and the first combined quarters. The dated
events that will show whether it’s working are in the Catalysts table
below.

Update, Nov 10, 2026: Since Oct 26, AIR has risen 19% to \$115.61, and
the combined company now trades at about 14.1x EBITDA, so part of the
re-rating has already happened. The thesis hasn’t changed, and my \$157
target still implies about 36% upside from here.

## Industry and Competition

AAR’s advantage is scale in a market short of hangar space and
mechanics. After the deal it will run about 19 million service hours a
year across 12 facilities, and airlines that send whole fleets to one
provider need that kind of capacity.

**Industry drivers:** The deal presentation projects the global fleet
growing from about 29,000 aircraft to 42,000 by 2035, roughly 4% a year.
Late deliveries from Boeing and Airbus keep older jets flying longer,
and older jets need more heavy checks and parts. Flight hours drive the
work, so air traffic matters more than new orders.

**Regulation and macro:** The 2024 FAA Reauthorization Act tightens
oversight of foreign repair stations, which matters for MRO’s Latin
American hangars. Jet fuel prices affect how long airlines keep older
planes, interest rates affect AAR’s floating-rate debt, and defense
budgets drive Government Solutions, 15% of sales.

**Moat:** Hangars, FAA certifications and trained mechanics take years
to build, as AAR’s own expansions in Oklahoma City and Miami show.
Fleet-wide maintenance contracts run for several years, and Parts Supply
adds multi-year distribution agreements with parts makers.

**Five forces**

- **Rivalry:** Moderate. Heavy maintenance is fragmented and won
  contract by contract. Tight capacity supports pricing today, but
  airlines rebid fleets every few years.

- **New entrants:** Low. Space, certification and skilled labor take
  years to build, and labor is the binding constraint.

- **Supplier power:** Moderate to high. Parts makers control
  distribution rights and can move them, and skilled U.S. mechanics are
  scarce.

- **Buyer power:** High. A handful of large airlines buy most heavy
  maintenance, award whole fleets at once, and can move work in-house.

- **Substitutes:** Moderate. Airlines’ own maintenance shops,
  alternative (PMA) parts instead of original ones, and new jets, which
  need little heavy maintenance in their early years.

## What Would Change My Mind

Right when: the combined EBITDA margin is near 16% in the first two
quarters after closing (10-Q); AAR converts more than 40% of EBITDA to
cash (quarterly adjusted operating cash flow); net leverage is below
3.0x by fiscal 2029 (10-K).

Wrong if: conversion stays near 28% through fiscal 2027 (trim); MRO’s
margin falls below 24% (sell); leverage isn’t on track for 3.0x by
fiscal 2029 (review the position).

Leg fit: AIR is the commercial aftermarket leg’s only holding. Older
fleets flying longer drive demand for heavy maintenance and used parts,
which is most of what AAR and MRO sell. Commercial customers were 72% of
AAR’s fiscal 2026 sales, and about 79% after adding MRO, whose customers
are mostly U.S. airlines.

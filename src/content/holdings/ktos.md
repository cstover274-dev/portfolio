Kratos Defense & Security Solutions (KTOS) hit an all-time stock high of
\$134.00 in January 2026 and has been in a steady decline ever since.
The market is half right when valuing KTOS. It is right about the demand
and the huge backlog it has acquired; it is also fixing a bottleneck the
whole industry faces. For decades, two companies made nearly all US
solid rocket motors. Kratos is among the new entrants trying to change
that. Where the market is wrong is the price of \$33.91 a share. In my personal portfolio I own 91 shares. My
valuation has the company sitting at \$27.60 a share, and I am firm on a
Hold rating.

## The Rally and the Fall

KTOS is down 74.7% from its high while its competitor AeroVironment
(AVAV) is down 71.2%, and the defense ETF is down 16.4%. The run-up came
from a higher multiple, not from higher earnings. The market value went
from \$1.5 billion to about \$22 billion while trailing EBITDA went from
\$45 million to \$90 million and the margin stayed near 5-6%.
AeroVironment’s rise followed up to \$1.5 billion of contract awards;
KTOS peaked at roughly 15 times its \$1.48 billion backlog. The street
mean target followed the price: \$74.21, then \$119.45, now \$74.68.

The fall was deserved, but it hasn’t made the stock cheaper. At \$33.91,
KTOS still trades at 21.8x 2027 EBITDA against a peer median near 18x.
So, what is KTOS building?

## Company Background

KTOS has its headquarters in Round Rock, Texas. Government Solutions
makes up 82% of revenue and Unmanned Systems the other 18%. Government
Solutions includes hypersonic systems, defense rocket support, small
turbine engines (Spartan), microwave electronics, space and satellite
ground systems, training, and C5ISR. Unmanned Systems includes the
Valkyrie tactical drone and Firejet. Products made up 63% of revenue and
services 37% in the first nine months of 2026. By contract type, revenue
is 67% fixed price, 28% cost plus and 4% time and material. Fixed price
contracts are a big liability for small and mid-sized defense
contractors. Unlike traditional cost reimbursement models where the
government absorbs any unexpected costs, fixed agreements lock in a
predetermined price. Foreign customers made up 19% of the nine-month
revenue; Israel was 11% of Q3 revenue. Revenue is earned mostly over
time on the cost-to-cost method, which is why its unbilled receivables
are large. Unbilled receivables are revenue that the company has earned
and recognized on its income statement but can’t yet invoice to the
customer. This ties up cash and can slow down investments and expansion.
U.S. Government sales accounted for 71% of nine-month revenue, or
\$928.8 million; this figure also includes foreign military sales
through the U.S. government.

Leadership has been stable. Eric M. DeMarco, the president and CEO,
joined in November 2003, became CEO in 2004 and took the company from a
wireless network service to a defense contractor. CFO Deanna Hom Lund
joined when Eric became CEO in 2004; both Eric and Deanna came from
Titan Corporation. Directors and officers as a group own 1.5% of the
shares. Eric owns 1,123,927 shares and Deanna holds 304,715 shares. The
group held about 813,000 fewer shares than a year earlier, and its stake
fell from 2.37% to 1.5%, partly because the 2026 stock sale raised the
share count. One division president adopted a plan in Q3 to sell up to
78,000 shares. Insiders own little of the company and held less this
year than last, so their interests ride more on pay than on the share
price.

Executive bonuses are weighted 25% adjusted EBITDA, 25% free cash flow,
10% revenue and 40% non-financial goals. The award mix is split between
50% performance-based and 50% time-based at target. Performance-based
RSUs vest 33.3% for every 10% increase in adjusted EBITDA over a 5-year
period. Since these grants began in 2020, adjusted EBITDA is up 55.1%
from 2019 as of December 28, 2025, about 7.6% a year. In 2025 Kratos
planned to use \$82.8 million of free cash flow and used \$125.4
million, and the CEO and CFO still received 75% of their target bonus.
The stock awards, the largest part of pay, depend only on adjusted
EBITDA growth. This relates directly back to my thesis; they were still
paid even when they missed the cash target.

## The Capacity Is Real

The demand behind the story is real. Backlog is \$2.65 billion against
\$1.48 billion a year ago, the Q3 book-to-bill was 2.2, and half the
backlog converts in 2027 against a \$2.1-2.2 billion revenue guide.
Revenue for 2027 is mostly spoken for, so the debate is about profit,
not sales.

Kratos is adding capacity in two places where the supply chain is short:
solid rocket motors and small turbojets. Kratos formed Prometheus, a
50/50 venture with RAFAEL, to build solid rocket motors at a new plant
in Indiana. First firing will happen in 2027 and production in 2028. The
Spartan engine line will build around 3,000 engines in 2027 and deliver
1,500 of them. Hypersonic revenue is estimated at \$700 million or more
in 2027 compared to \$200 million in 2025.

These programs move the share price less than the headlines suggest.
Prometheus is worth about \$0.41 a share on my assumptions and Valkyrie
timing about \$0.30. The Valkyrie ramp to 40 a year has slipped. Two
other items get attention but sit only in my bull case. An OpenSpace
contract win would add about \$75 million a year by 2029, and a royalty
on Taiwan-built units about \$6 million a year. Neither changes the
picture: together they’re under 4% of revenue. KTOS isn’t the only
company scaling turbojets. The capacity is real. But does it earn a
margin?

## The Margin Question

Management promises about a point of margin a year and hasn’t shown
where it comes from. Adjusted EBITDA margin is guided to 9.9% for 2026
and 10.9% for 2027. In my model nearly all the gain comes from SG&A
falling as a share of revenue (+4.4 points), not from gross margin. SG&A
falls from 19.4% of revenue to 15%.

[[chart:ktos-bridge]]

*Nearly all the margin gain to 2036 comes from SG&A, not gross margin.*

The mix isn’t getting richer. Unmanned earns a 17.9% gross margin and a
0.6% segment operating margin and lost \$1.0 million in Q3. Government
Solutions earns a 23.8% gross margin, 67% of revenue is fixed price, and
the legacy drone lots can’t be repriced until the next lot. That leaves
cost cutting as the only route to get to the margin target.

Part of the reported margin is a matter of definition. \$16.5 million of
the \$127.8 million nine-month adjusted EBITDA is an add-back for
amortized contract costs; on my definition the figure is \$115.1
million. The margin peaked near 12% in 2012 and has run 5-8% since. Even
in the bull case, with gross margin at 25.4% and SG&A at 13%, the EBITDA
margin is still 12.7%. If margins stay near 10%, can Kratos fund the
build-out it has planned?

## Cash and Payback

KTOS won’t run out of money. It raised \$1,348 million net in 2026 and
holds about \$1.3 billion. In my model, counting stock compensation and
Prometheus funding as costs, cumulative free cash flow bottoms near
negative \$375 million in 2029. So, the risk isn’t bankruptcy; it’s
another share sale at a low price.

The problem is how long shareholders must wait to be paid back.
Cumulative free cash flow turns positive in 2033 and reaches about \$440
million by 2036, roughly 6% of today’s market value. Returns stay below
the cost of capital: a 5.9% ROIC against a 9.46% WACC.

[[chart:ktos-fcf]]

The cash is going into working capital as much as into factories.
Operating cash flow was negative \$43.6 million for the nine months,
after \$180.7 million went into working capital. Unbilled receivables
rose 55% to \$454.3 million while revenue grew 30%, and capex was \$67.3
million. What would raise the stakes: a \$500 million raise near \$34
would add about 14.7 million shares, a 7.7% dilution. So, what is a
business like that worth?

## The Bulls Are Right About Growth, Not About Margins

On growth, Wall Street is right: revenue roughly doubles from \$1.04
billion in 2023 to about \$2.2 billion on 2027 guidance, backed by a
\$2.65 billion backlog and a Q3 book-to-bill of 2.2. This growth was
mostly organic. Next year is largely booked, with about \$1.33 billion
of 2027 revenue already in the backlog, roughly 60% of the guide’s
midpoint, with a \$15.5 billion pipeline behind it. Hypersonics drives
much of it, from \$200 million of revenue in 2025 to a targeted \$700
million or more in 2027, plus turbojets and Firejet. My model has 2027
revenue at \$2.24 billion, which is slightly above guidance, so my
disagreement with the street isn’t about sales.

Where I differ from the street is on what growth will earn. The street’s
2027 margin matches management’s 10.9% guide, and my estimate is not far
off. The real gap is what the street target assumes. The \$74.68 mean
target values Kratos at about 56 times 2027 EBITDA, while peers trade at
15-20x. Even today’s price requires a free cash flow margin of about 18%
by 2036, against 3% in my model. Kratos’s EBITDA margin hasn’t been
above 12% since 2012.

Three factors hold the margin down, and more volume doesn’t fix any of
them. Two-thirds of revenue is fixed price, so cost overruns land on
Kratos, and the drone business earns almost nothing today. The
improvement I do model comes from spreading overhead, not from selling
more profitable products. And even reported profit doesn’t all become
cash: stock compensation runs about \$85 million a year, and unbilled
receivables keep absorbing working capital. Even with generous
assumptions, my bull case only reaches a 12.7% margin and every value it
produces is below today’s price. The mean target has continued to fall
from \$119.45 in March to \$74.68 now, while 24 of 25 analysts kept
their Buy ratings. As 2027 margins land at guidance rather than above
it, I expect targets to keep coming down even if ratings don’t.

## At 21.8x, the Stock Still Prices Peer Margins Kratos Hasn’t Earned

I value Kratos using the EV to 2027 EBITDA multiple, because Kratos has
net cash and little GAAP earnings, so using the P/E multiple doesn’t
work. To get to a share price, I take enterprise value, add \$1,324
million of cash, subtract \$188 million of finance leases and divide by
190.3 million diluted shares. My EBITDA consists of operating income
plus D&A, stock compensation and deal costs. The company’s definition
adds back amortized contract costs. Using the company’s definition would
add \$1.32 a share, which puts the value at about \$28.80, still 15%
below what it is trading at today.

At 21.8x, KTOS trades above every peer I keep. Peers run 15.3x to 19.9x
with a median of 18.5x on margins of roughly 14-23% against about 11%
for KTOS. Kratos is the fastest grower in the group; it guides revenue
up around 18-22%. Kratos has always traded at a premium, but the premium
was never backed by margin growth.

I value Kratos at around \$27.60 a share, which is 19% below today’s
price. I got there using two different methods. First, I valued each
segment at what its peers trade for, 18.2x for Government Solutions and
19.5x for Unmanned. That gives me \$27.69 a share. Second, I applied the
peer group median multiple to the whole company, which gave me \$27.48 a
share. The two methods land 21 cents apart, so the answer doesn’t depend
on which one I pick.

The DCF model comes in about \$11.50 at a 9.46% WACC and 3% terminal
growth. Peers trade at 18x today; the DCF’s terminal value works out to
about 5x 2036 EBITDA. The floor is sitting at about \$6 a share of net
cash. The multiple is what the market pays today at \$27.60 a share, and
the DCF is what the cash flow supports at \$11.50. With a 12x exit, the
DCF gives \$11.35 in the bear case, \$18.14 in the base case and \$28.42
in the bull case. Even my bull case is below today’s price of \$33.91.

[[chart:ktos-valuation]]

My choice leans conservative in two places. A stricter EBITDA definition
is worth about \$1.32 a share and the DCF counts stock compensation as a
cost, which most street models don’t. A 20% EBITDA margin by 2036 with a
14x exit supports today’s \$33.91, and some peers already earn that
margin. Kratos has never been above 12%, so 20% is a long way to go and
is going to be hard to prove.

## The Risks Center on Whether Revenue Turns into Cash

Fixed price contracts: Two-thirds of Kratos’s revenue is fixed-price, so
when labor or materials cost more, Kratos absorbs it. The 10-Q says this
is already hurting margins on drone contracts priced in 2020 and 2021,
which can’t be repriced until the next production lot. Each point of
2027 EBITDA margin is worth about \$2.15 a share, so a small miss
matters. The offset is that estimate changes have been immaterial so
far, and management expects better pricing on the next lots.

Cash conversion: Unbilled receivables rose 55% to \$454.3 million in
nine months while revenue grew 30%, and DSO is sitting at 125 days. If
unbilled days stay at 95, it costs about \$1.00 a share (\$11.49 to
\$10.47 on the DCF). Part of the rise came with the Nomad and Orbit
deals, and milestone billings should release some of it.

Budget and timing: The government is running on a continuing resolution,
which delays new contract starts. Kratos has guided the first quarter of
2027 as its lightest and said it would reassess its full-year guide if
the resolution runs past March 31, 2027. A cut to that guide is my bear
case, which lowers my value by about \$1.50 a share (\$27.69 base to
\$26.15 bear). The offset is that about \$1.33 billion of 2027 revenue
is already in Kratos’s backlog, so most of the year doesn’t depend on a
new award.

Dilution: On Kratos’s own definition, free cash flow is a \$110-120
million use in 2026 and improves to about a \$30 million use in 2027 in
my model. But that adds back about \$85 million of stock compensation
and leaves out \$40 million for Prometheus. Counting both, 2027 uses
about \$180 million. A \$500 million raise at \$34 would add about 14.7
million new shares, 7.7% dilution. The offset: about \$1.3 billion of
cash covers the build-out in my base case.

Execution: The Valkyrie ramp to 40 a year has been pushed out, and
Prometheus doesn’t produce until 2028; 3,000 turbojets are being built
ahead of contracts. Prometheus is worth about \$0.41 a share and the
Valkyrie timing about \$0.30 a share. Low-rate Valkyrie awards are in
hand, and Firejet demand is filling up the plant.

The risk to my call: Margins improve faster than I expect. If February’s
guidance puts the 2027 adjusted EBITDA margin at 12% or better, or free
cash flow turns positive in 2027, my value is too low and I’d raise it.
Each turn of EV/EBITDA is worth about \$1.16 a share, so a re-rating to
the 24x the stock held earlier would add roughly \$7.

## Bottom Line

Kratos is building real capacity, and the stock still prices in margins
it hasn’t earned. The rally was higher multiple, not higher earnings:
market value rose about fifteenfold while EBITDA doubled. The mix isn’t
getting any richer, with Unmanned at a 0.6% operating margin and 67% of
revenue fixed price. Cumulative free cash flow doesn’t turn positive
until 2033 after stock compensation, which is a major concern for
investors. I value it at \$27.60 a share, 19% below today’s price, and
even my bull case doesn’t reach \$33.91. The floor is \$6 a share of net
cash. I’m holding rather than selling because the stock could re-rate if
February’s guidance shows margins improving.

I’ll revisit when Kratos reports Q4 and gives 2027 guidance in late
February. If adjusted EBITDA margin reaches 12% with a path higher, or
free cash flow turns positive in 2027, I’d consider an upgrade. If
Kratos sells more stock before free cash flow turns positive, I’d trim
or sell. The budget standoff, contract headlines and swings in
drone-stock sentiment are noise. The full list of signals is in What
Would Change My Mind.

**Update, November 17, 2026:** Since my November 13 research date, KTOS has fallen 8.4% to \$31.06. It now trades at about 19.6x 2027 EBITDA, still above the 18.5x peer median. Nothing in the thesis has changed, and my \$27.60 value implies about 11% downside from here.

## Industry and Competition

Kratos’s advantage is affordable mass: building high-performance,
rapidly deployable military systems at a fraction of the cost and the
development time. Rather than waiting for the government to give it a
contract for researching new technology, it funds its own research so it
can get the hardware to the market first.

**Industry drivers**: Kratos has carved out a high-growth niche in a
defense industry that is being reshaped by record military budgets,
acquisition reforms, and a strategic shift toward low-cost autonomous
warfare. It self-funds R&D, makes its own small jet engines and builds
hardware ahead of formal orders. Kratos positions itself as faster and
cheaper than the large primes, and in propulsion it also sells to them:
in July 2026 it delivered turbomachinery for Lockheed Martin’s ramjet
program. Against newer defense-tech startups, its factories and decades
of flight history give it a track record they don’t have yet.

**Regulation and Macro:** Kratos is heavily exposed to federal budget
delays, fixed-price inflation squeezes and strict defense export rules.
A government shutdown would delay orders. Kratos and other firms face an
industry-wide shortage of solid rocket motors and rare earth minerals.
Lastly, there is a tight market for aerospace engineers and machinists
with U.S. security clearances, which pushes up the cost of keeping them.

**Moat**: Kratos possesses a narrow to moderate moat rooted in high
switching costs, proprietary hardware and a strict regulatory barrier
rather than traditional monopoly scale. Once Kratos’s electronics,
rocket systems or small jet turbines are designed into a Pentagon
platform, the cost and risk of re-engineering and recertifying a
replacement locks in sole-source revenue for years, according to the
10-K. This advantage is reinforced by its in-house jet engine
manufacturing and decades of proven flight success. These are barriers
that newer defense tech startups struggle to match quickly. But the moat
is limited by fierce fixed-price competition from both legacy primes and
well-funded autonomous software rivals. And Kratos’s margins have never
shown pricing power: they trail every peer’s.

**Five forces**

**Rivalry**: High. Kratos competes against massive legacy defense primes
that possess vast balance sheets and political influence as well as
agile, venture-backed defense tech startups that lead in autonomous AI
software.

**New entrants**: Moderate. Defense work requires a facility security
clearance, cleared staff, strict compliance and years of flight testing.
Well-funded startups have cleared these hurdles, so the barrier slows
entry rather than stopping it.

**Supplier power**: High. The aerospace industry faces tight supply for
specialized castings, microelectronics, rare earth minerals, and solid
rocket motors, giving niche component vendors pricing leverage and
causing delivery delays. U.S. rules favor domestic suppliers for many
defense parts and materials, with some allied countries allowed, which
narrows the supplier pool.

**Buyer power**: High. Kratos relies on the U.S. government and allied
militaries for most of its revenue, giving the Pentagon immense leverage
over contract terms, pricing audits, and delivery schedules.

**Substitutes**: Moderate. Traditional manned aircraft and legacy
hardware face high substitution threats from low-cost drones and
software-defined systems, a trend that actually benefits Kratos as a
disruptor.
